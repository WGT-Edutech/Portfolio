THE 4 CRITERIA

What Examiners Actually Score

Every IELTS essay is marked on four criteria, each worth 25% of your Writing score. Understanding these is the single most important thing you can do.

1. Task Response

Do you answer every part of the question? Is your position clear throughout? Are your ideas fully developed with relevant support?

2. Coherence & Cohesion

Is your essay logically organised? Do paragraphs have clear central topics? Are linking devices used appropriately (not mechanically)?

3. Lexical Resource

Do you use a wide range of vocabulary with flexibility? Are word choices precise? Can you paraphrase naturally? Spelling and collocation matter.

4. Grammatical Range & Accuracy

Do you use a variety of complex structures? Are errors rare and minor? Punctuation counts. Band 7+ requires frequent error-free sentences.

CRITERION 1 — 25% OF SCORE

Task Response: The Make-or-Break Criterion

This is where most candidates lose the most marks. You can have beautiful English but still score Band 6 if you don't address the task properly.

What Band 9 Looks Like

  • Addresses all parts of the task fully
  • Presents a clear, consistent position throughout
  • Develops ideas with extended, well-supported arguments
  • Avoids over-generalisation — every claim has specific support

Common Pitfalls (Band 5–6 Traps)

  • ✗ Addressing only part of the question
  • ✗ Giving a position but not sustaining it through the essay
  • ✗ Writing general statements without examples or explanation
  • ✗ Going off-topic — writing everything you know about the subject

Examiner's mindset: "Does this candidate answer the specific question asked — or are they reciting a memorised essay?"

CRITERION 2 — 25% OF SCORE

Coherence & Cohesion: The Reader's Experience

Coherence = logical flow of ideas. Cohesion = how sentences and paragraphs connect. Examiners should never have to re-read a sentence to understand your argument.

Paragraph Structure (Every Body Paragraph)

1. Topic sentence — states the main idea

2. Explanation — why is this true?

3. Example / evidence — specific support

4. Link back to your position / thesis

Signposting

Use clear topic sentences at the start of each paragraph. The examiner should know your essay's structure from reading only the first sentence of each paragraph.

Linking Devices: The Right Way

✓ Use linkers naturally — they should clarify meaning, not decorate

✓ Vary your devices: however, although, while, whereas, despite, nevertheless

✗ Don't start every sentence with a linker

✗ Don't use "Furthermore" five times in one essay

✗ Don't use linkers that are too informal (besides, anyway, stuff like that)

Band 9 cohesion is often invisible — ideas flow logically without relying on obvious linking words.

CRITERION 3 — 25% OF SCORE

Lexical Resource: Vocabulary That Scores

It's not about using big words. It's about using the right words, in the right combinations, with precision.

What Examiners Reward

✓ Topic-specific vocabulary — words relevant to the question

✓ Collocations — natural word pairings (e.g. pose a threat, not make a threat)

✓ Paraphrasing skill — restating ideas in different words

✓ Less common vocabulary — used naturally, not forced

✓ Spelling accuracy — even Band 9 allows occasional slips, but they must be rare

CRITERION 4 — 25% OF SCORE

Grammatical Range & Accuracy

You need to demonstrate control of a variety of complex structures. It's not enough to be accurate with simple sentences — you must show you can handle complexity.

Structures Examiners Look For

✓ Complex sentences — subordinate clauses, relative clauses

✓ Conditionals — Were governments to invest… / If this trend were to continue…

✓ Passive voice — used appropriately (It has been argued that…)

✓ Participle clauses — Having considered both sides, I believe…

✓ Inversion — Not only does this… but it also…

✓ Modal verbs — may, might, could, would, should (nuanced usage)

✓ Varied sentence length — mix of short punchy sentences and longer complex ones

Band Score Comparison

BandGrammar Profile
5Limited range. Frequent errors. Meaning sometimes unclear.
6Mix of simple and complex. Errors occur but rarely impede communication.

Key insight: Band 7+ requires frequent complex structures — not just one or two.

ESSAY ARCHITECTURE

The 4-Paragraph Structure (Task 2)

This template works for all Task 2 question types: Opinion, Discussion, Problem-Solution, and Two-Part questions.

Introduction (2–3 sentences)

Sentence 1: Paraphrase the topic (don't copy the question)

Sentence 2: State your position / thesis (for opinion essays) or outline what you'll discuss

~40 words

Body Paragraph 1 (4–5 sentences)

Topic sentence → Explain → Example → Link

~80–100 words

Body Paragraph 2 (4–5 sentences)

Topic sentence → Explain → Example → Link

~80–100 words

Conclusion (2–3 sentences)

Sentence 1: Summarise your main argument (in different words from the introduction)

Sentence 2: Final thought / recommendation / prediction

~30–40 words. NEVER introduce new ideas.

Word count target: 270–300 words. Under 250 = automatic penalty. Over 330 = wasted time and more error opportunities.

BAND 9 MODEL ESSAY #1 — OPINION

Sample: Technology & Education

Question: "Some people believe that technology has made education more effective. Others argue that it has created new problems. Discuss both views and give your own opinion."

Band 9 Response (~285 words)

The role of technology in education has become a subject of considerable debate. While some contend that digital tools have revolutionised learning, others maintain that they have introduced new challenges. I believe that, despite certain drawbacks, technology has fundamentally enhanced the educational experience.

On the one hand, critics of technology in education raise valid concerns. The proliferation of digital devices has been linked to diminished attention spans, with students increasingly unable to engage in sustained, deep reading. Furthermore, the digital divide means that students from disadvantaged backgrounds may lack access to the same resources, thereby exacerbating existing inequalities. These are not trivial concerns; they warrant serious consideration from policymakers.

On the other hand, the benefits of educational technology are substantial and far-reaching. Interactive platforms such as Khan Academy and Coursera have democratised access to high-quality instruction, enabling learners in remote areas to study subjects that would otherwise be unavailable to them. Moreover, adaptive learning software can tailor content to individual students' needs, a level of personalisation that traditional classroom teaching simply cannot achieve. Research from Stanford University has consistently shown that blended learning approaches — combining online and face-to-face instruction — yield superior outcomes compared to purely traditional methods.

In my view, the advantages of technology in education significantly outweigh the disadvantages. The key lies not in rejecting technology but in implementing it thoughtfully, with adequate training for teachers and equitable access for students. When deployed strategically, digital tools have the potential to transform education in ways that were unimaginable a generation ago.

Why this scores Band 9: Clear position throughout · Both views fully developed with specific examples · Sophisticated vocabulary (proliferation, democratised, adaptive, deployed strategically) · Complex grammar (participle clauses, conditionals, relative clauses) · Cohesive without over-reliance on obvious linkers · ~285 words

BAND 9 MODEL ESSAY #2 — PROBLEM/SOLUTION

Sample: Urbanisation & Environment

Question: "In many cities, the movement of people from rural areas has led to environmental problems. What are these problems? What can be done to address them?"

Band 9 Response (~275 words)

The mass migration of people from rural to urban areas has placed enormous pressure on cities' environments. This essay will examine the primary environmental consequences of rapid urbanisation and propose several measures to mitigate them.

The most pressing environmental problem associated with urban migration is air pollution. As cities expand, the number of vehicles on roads increases dramatically, releasing harmful emissions that degrade air quality and pose serious public health risks. In cities such as Delhi and Beijing, air pollution levels regularly exceed World Health Organisation guidelines by a factor of ten or more. Additionally, the construction of new housing and infrastructure often leads to deforestation and the destruction of natural habitats, which in turn reduces biodiversity and disrupts local ecosystems. The strain on water resources is equally concerning; many rapidly growing cities face chronic water shortages as demand outstrips supply.

Addressing these challenges requires a multifaceted approach. First, governments should invest heavily in public transportation systems — electric buses, metro networks, and cycling infrastructure — to reduce dependence on private vehicles. Cities like Copenhagen and Amsterdam have demonstrated that such investments can dramatically lower emissions while improving quality of life. Second, urban planning regulations must incorporate green spaces and protect existing natural areas within city boundaries. Singapore's "City in a Garden" model provides an excellent template for integrating nature into urban environments. Finally, water recycling and conservation measures should be mandated for all new developments.

In conclusion, while urbanisation poses significant environmental threats, these can be effectively managed through strategic investment in sustainable infrastructure and forward-thinking urban planning.

Why this scores Band 9: All parts of task addressed · Specific real-world examples (Delhi, Beijing, Copenhagen, Singapore) · Wide vocabulary (multifaceted, chronic, mandated, template) · Range of complex structures · Logical paragraphing · Clear problem → solution progression

AVOID THESE

Common Mistakes That Cap Your Score

Task Response Errors

✗ Writing a memorised essay regardless of the question

✗ Only addressing one part of a two-part question

✗ Giving a position in the intro then contradicting it

✗ Writing everything you know about the topic instead of answering the question

Vocabulary Errors

✗ Using informal language (kids, stuff, gonna, a lot of)

✗ Overusing the same words — no paraphrasing

✗ Using big words incorrectly — this lowers your score

✗ Spelling errors in common academic words

Grammar Errors

✗ Only writing simple sentences — no complex structures

�span> Run-on sentences — multiple ideas joined by commas

✗ Article errors (a/an/the) — very common, very costly

✗ Subject-verb disagreement in complex sentences

Structural Errors

✗ No paragraphs — one block of text

✗ Too short — under 250 words (Task 2) or 150 (Task 1)

✗ Introducing new ideas in the conclusion

✗ Copying the question word-for-word in your introduction

Golden rule: If you wouldn't say it in an academic essay at university, don't write it in IELTS.

EXAM DAY STRATEGY

Your 40-Minute Game Plan (Task 2)

Minutes 1–5: Analyse & Plan

  • Read the question twice
  • Identify the question type (opinion, discussion, etc.)
  • Underline key words and task instructions
  • Brainstorm 2–3 ideas for each body paragraph
  • Decide your position (if required)

Minutes 6–10: Write Introduction

  • Paraphrase the topic
  • State your thesis / outline
  • Keep it concise — 2 sentences maximum

Minutes 11–25: Write Body Paragraphs

  • Follow the TEEL structure (Topic → Explain → Example → Link)
  • One idea per paragraph — don't mix arguments
  • Use a range of vocabulary and grammar
  • Include specific examples (real or plausible)

Minutes 26–35: Conclusion + Review

  • Summarise in different words from the intro
  • Never add new ideas
  • Check: Did I answer every part of the question?
  • Check: Is my position clear throughout?
  • Check: Spelling, grammar, word count

KEY TAKEAWAYS

What Examiners Reward

1. Answer the specific question — not the topic in general

2. Maintain a clear position throughout (for opinion essays)

3. Develop every idea with explanation + specific example

4. Use varied, precise vocabulary — not just "big words"

5. Demonstrate complex grammar frequently and accurately

6. Organise ideas logically — one idea per paragraph

The examiner reads hundreds of essays.
Make yours clear, specific, and well-structured — and you'll stand out.
